The Mathematics

The round robin is a one-factorization of Kn

Model the field as the complete graph Kn: one vertex per player, one edge for every required match. A legal round is a set of matches in which nobody appears twice. In graph terms that is a perfect matching, also called a one-factor: no player is double booked. A schedule that plays every edge exactly once therefore partitions the edge set of Kn into rounds, which is a one-factorization. The circle method constructs one directly: fix one seat, rotate everyone else one seat per round, and pair opposite seats.

The object
Why every pair meets exactly once, in two steps

Number the ring vertices 1 to n-1 in entry order around the fixed vertex. Step (1) is read off the seating geometry: opposite seats have constant position sum, and each rotation shifts every position by one, so the sum class the round selects moves by -2. Step (2) is the arithmetic that makes the sweep exhaustive. Disjointness is the verifier's no repeat check; totality is the edge counter reaching n(n-1)/2. Kirkman first constructed one-factorizations (1847); the circle arrangement itself appears in Lucas (1883).

Race calibration

Every simulated match draws its winner from exactly these odds. The race score model then finds the per rack probability whose race outcome matches E, so favorites usually win, upsets stay possible, and scorelines look like real pool.

One round, one perfect matching

Vertices are the players, fixed in place; the ringed vertex is the fixed seat, and its brighter chord is that player's match, the clock hand of the rotation. Bold chords are this round's matching Mr: no two share a vertex, and every vertex is hit exactly once, which is why all the dots pulse as a round lands. Stepping forward slides each chord one ring seat, the rotation itself, while played edges stay behind as chalk residue until every edge of Kn is drawn exactly once. A dashed chord is a bye.

Placement recurrence

A documented limitation of the circle method

If B meets A immediately after A faced C, B is said to receive a carry-over effect from C, for example facing an opponent right after a bruising loss. Build the matrix G where G[j][k] counts how often some player handed a carry-over from j to k, then measure imbalance by the sum of squares of its entries. A balanced schedule spreads the handoffs so every off-diagonal entry is 1, reaching the minimum n(n-1).

The circle method does the opposite: it attains the maximum possible carry-over value, and every schedule with maximum carry-over arises from the circle method. The heatmap is this field's matrix, and the value is computed live below. For a student union tournament the trade for simplicity is acceptable, but fairer constructions exist, such as Russell's designs, balanced whenever n is a power of two.
