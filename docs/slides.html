1 · Rating players

Elo expectation

EA = 11 + 10(RB − RA) / 400

Read it aloud"E of A equals one, divided by one plus ten, raised to the power of R B minus R A, over four hundred."

What it meansYour win chance depends only on the rating gap. A 400 point gap makes you ten times more likely to win. Equal ratings give exactly one half.

Real numbers1650 vs 1500 gives E = 0.703: the stronger player wins about 70% of matches.

1 · Rating players

Elo update

R′ = R + K(S − E),   K = 32

Read it aloud"R new equals R old, plus K times S minus E. K is thirty two, and S is one for a win, zero for a loss."

What it meansYou gain K times how surprising the result was. The winner's gain equals the loser's loss, so rating points are conserved. Upsets pay more, because when E was small, S minus E is nearly one.

Real numbersAn underdog at E = 0.389 who wins gets 32 × 0.611 = +19.6. A favorite winning the same match gets only +12.4.

1 · Rating players

The race model

P(N, q) = N−1Σk=0 C(N−1+k, k)   qN (1−q)k

Read it aloud"P of N and q equals the sum, for k from zero to N minus one, of N minus one plus k choose k, times q to the N, times one minus q to the k."

What it meansThe chance of winning a race to N racks when each rack is a coin with probability q. I solve it backwards by bisection: find the q whose match odds equal the Elo odds exactly, instead of letting a long race inflate the favorite.

Real numbersE = 0.703 gives q = 0.586. Using 0.703 directly would blow the match odds up to 0.905.

2 · Counting the event

Handshake lemma

|E(Kn)| = 12 Σv deg(v) = n(n−1)2

Read it aloud"The number of edges of K n equals one half the sum of all the vertex degrees, which comes out to n times n minus one, over two."

What it meansPlayers are vertices, matches are edges. Each of the n players has n minus one opponents, and dividing by two stops us counting every match from both ends.

Real numbers8 players: 28 matches. 30 players: 435 matches.

2 · Counting the event

Real event duration

racks ≈ N + N−12,   time = rounds × ⌈matches per roundtables⌉ × racks × minutes per rack

Read it aloud"Racks is about N plus half of N minus one. Time equals rounds, times matches per round over tables rounded up, times racks per match, times minutes per rack."

What it meansThe winner plays exactly N racks and the loser averages about half of N minus one, so a race to five runs about seven racks. Rounds are sequential, and matches share the Student Union's six tables in waves.

Real numbers8 players: about 6.5 hours, one long evening. 30 players: over 80 hours. Small fields fit a night; big fields are simulation territory.

3 · Scheduling

A schedule is a one-factorization

E(Kn) = M1 ∪ M2 ∪ … ∪ Mn−1

Read it aloud"The edge set of K n splits into M one through M n minus one: disjoint perfect matchings, one per round."

What it meansA round is played simultaneously, so nobody appears twice: that is a perfect matching. A full schedule uses every edge exactly once, so it chops the whole graph into perfect matchings. Scheduling a round robin IS one-factorizing the complete graph.

Real numbersK6: 15 edges, 5 rounds of 3 matches, 5 × 3 = 15.

3 · Scheduling · the one to own

The circle method works because 2 is invertible

i + j ≡ −2r  (mod n−1)

Read it aloud"i plus j is congruent to minus two r, mod n minus one."

What it meansPair across the ring, rotate one seat per round. Opposite seats share a position sum, and each rotation shifts it by minus two. Since n is even, n minus one is odd, so two is invertible, and the rounds sweep every residue class exactly once. Each pair has one fixed sum, so each pair meets exactly once.

3 · Scheduling

Chromatic index: why byes exist

χ′(Kn) = n−1  (n even),    n  (n odd)

Read it aloud"The chromatic index of K n is n minus one when n is even, and n when n is odd."

What it meansA schedule is an edge coloring, one color per round. Even fields color perfectly. Odd fields need one extra round, and every round leaves exactly one player uncolored: that is the graph theory reason byes exist. The app adds a phantom seat, so everyone rests exactly once.

Real numbers13 players: 13 rounds, one rotating bye per round.

4 · The knockout

Seeding by the doubling recurrence

S(1) = [1],   S(2k) = [s,  2k+1−s] for each s ∈ S(k)

Read it aloud"S of one is just seed one. To double the bracket, each seed s gets paired with its mirror, two k plus one minus s."

What it meansS(8) comes out 1, 8, 4, 5, 2, 7, 3, 6: every first round pair sums to nine, strongest against weakest. The top seeds land in different subtrees, and two players can only meet at the root of the smallest subtree containing both. That is why seeds one and two can only meet in the final.

4 · The knockout

Match counts, by counting losses

single: k−1,    double: 2(k−1) + {0, 1}

Read it aloud"Single elimination is k minus one matches. Double elimination is two times k minus one, plus zero or one: so two k minus two, or two k minus one when the grand final resets."

What it meansCount losses, not matches: every match creates exactly one loss. Single elimination must eliminate k minus one players once. In double elimination every non champion exits with exactly two losses, and the reset game appears when the losers champion wins the first grand final.

Real numbersk = 8: single is 7 matches, double is 14, or 15 with a reset.

5 · An honest limitation

Carry-over imbalance

Γ = Σj,k G[j][k]2,    minimum = n(n−1)

Read it aloud"Gamma equals the sum, over all pairs j and k, of G at j k squared. The balanced minimum is n times n minus one."

What it meansG counts opponent handoffs between consecutive rounds: playing someone right after they faced a bruising opponent is a carry-over. The sum of squares measures how unevenly those pile up. A 2018 theorem proves the circle method is the worst possible schedule by this measure. I show that on purpose: the method with the one line proof costs carry-over balance, and stating the tradeoff is part of the design.

Real numbersK6: circle method Γ = 60, balanced minimum 30, exactly twice as bad. Balanced schedules exist when n is a power of two (Russell 1980).
