All the Math, One Page

Every equation in the project, in the order it appears in your 10 minute demo. Each one: the equation, how to say it, where it is on screen, and the real numbers.

Part 1: while the first tournament runs (standings and the Live Elo card)

Equation 1 Elo expectation eloExpected
EA = 11 + 10(RB − RA) / 400

Read it aloud: "E of A equals one, divided by one plus ten, raised to the power of R B minus R A, over four hundred."

Say it: "The chance A beats B depends only on the rating gap. Every 400 points of gap makes you 10 times more likely to win. Equal ratings give exactly one half."

On screen: the Live Elo update card under Standings prints this with real ratings after every match.

Real numbers: 1650 vs 1500: E = 1 / (1 + 10(1500 − 1650)/400) = 1 / (1 + 10−0.375) = 0.703. The stronger player should win about 70% of matches.

Equation 2 Elo update eloDelta
R′ = R + K(S − E),   K = 32,   S ∈ {0, 1}

Read it aloud: "R new equals R old, plus K times S minus E. K is thirty two, and S is one for a win, zero for a loss."

Say it: "You gain or lose K times how surprising the result was. S is 1 for a win, 0 for a loss. The winner's gain equals the loser's loss, so rating points are conserved, the system only moves points, never creates them."

On screen: the second line of the Live Elo card, and the green and red deltas beside the Elo column in Standings.

Real numbers: in my test run Omar (E = 0.389, the underdog) beat Marcus: ΔR = 32 × (1 − 0.389) = +19.6. A favorite winning the same match would only get 32 × (1 − 0.611) = +12.4. Upsets pay more. That is the whole matchmaking philosophy: beginners and pros both get fair, informative games.

Equation 3 The race model (negative binomial tail) raceWinProbability, simulateMatch
P(N, q) = N−1Σk=0 C(N−1+k, k)   qN (1−q)k

Read it aloud: "P of N and q equals the sum, for k from zero to N minus one, of N minus one plus k choose k, times q to the N, times one minus q to the k."

Say it: "A match is a race to N racks, first to 5, the final to 7. If each rack is a coin with probability q, this sums over every way to win: you take N racks while your opponent takes k, fewer than N, and the last rack is always yours. The catch: a long race amplifies a small edge. So I do NOT plug the Elo expectation in as q. I go backwards: bisection finds the q whose match odds equal the Elo odds exactly."

On screen: the Elo panel in the sidebar, and every scoreline the simulation produces.

Real numbers: for E = 0.703, bisection gives q = 0.586. Check: P(5, 0.586) = 0.703 exactly. If I had lazily used q = 0.703, the match odds would inflate to 0.905, punishing the underdog far beyond what the ratings say. So the favorite wins 70% of matches while winning only 59% of racks: realistic pool, close racks, occasional upsets.

Part 2: verification panel and the duration line

Equation 4 Handshake lemma (total matches) verifySchedule check 5
|E(Kn)| = 12 Σv deg(v) = n(n−1)2

Read it aloud: "The number of edges of K n equals one half the sum of all the vertex degrees, which comes out to n times n minus one, over two."

Say it: "Model players as vertices, matches as edges: everyone plays everyone means the complete graph. Each of the n players has n minus 1 opponents, and dividing by two because each match is counted from both ends gives n times n minus 1 over 2."

On screen: the Group Stage note ("28 matches in total") and verification check five.

Real numbers: n = 8: 8 × 7 / 2 = 28. n = 30: 30 × 29 / 2 = 435.

Equation 5 Real event duration expectedRacks, estimateEventMinutes
racks ≈ N + N−12,    time = rounds × ⌈matches per roundtables⌉ × racks × minutes per rack

Read it aloud: "Racks is about N plus half of N minus one. Time equals rounds, times matches per round over tables rounded up, times racks per match, times minutes per rack."

Say it: "The winner of a race to 5 plays exactly 5 racks; the loser averages about 2. So about 7 racks, roughly 56 minutes at 8 minutes a rack. Rounds run one after another, and inside a round the matches share the Union's six tables in waves."

On screen: the sentence at the end of the Group Stage note.

Real numbers: 8 players: 7 rounds × 1 wave × 56 min = 392 min ≈ 6.5 hours. 30 players: 29 rounds × 3 waves × 56 min = 4872 min ≈ 81 hours. Your line: "small fields fit an evening, big fields are simulation territory."

Part 3: the walkthrough, Act 1 (the heart: the circle method)

Equation 6 A schedule is a one-factorization
E(Kn) = M1 ∪ M2 ∪ … ∪ Mn−1

Read it aloud: "The edge set of K n splits into M one through M n minus one: disjoint perfect matchings, one per round."

Say it: "One round is played simultaneously, so nobody appears twice: that is a perfect matching of the graph. A full schedule chops ALL the edges into perfect matchings, one per round. That partition is called a one-factorization. So scheduling a fair round robin IS one-factorizing the complete graph."

On screen: walkthrough Act 1, the K6 diagram inking one matching per rotation.

Real numbers: K6 has 15 edges, 5 rounds of 3 matches: 5 × 3 = 15, every edge used exactly once.

Equation 7 THE proof (own this one) buildSchedule + the congruence test
i + j ≡ −2r (mod n−1)

Read it aloud: "i plus j is congruent to minus two r, mod n minus one."

Say it, four sentences, unaided: "Fix one player, put the other n minus 1 on a ring, pair across the ring, rotate one seat per round. Opposite seats have a constant position sum, and each rotation shifts that sum by minus 2, so players i and j meet in round r exactly when i plus j is congruent to minus 2r. Because n is even, n minus 1 is odd, so 2 is invertible mod n minus 1, and r maps to minus 2r hits every residue class exactly once. Each pair has one fixed sum, so each pair meets in exactly one round."

On screen: the congruence table in the walkthrough, generated live from the shipped scheduler.

Real numbers (n = 6, ring players 1 to 5, fixed player 0):

round r−2r mod 5ring pairs with that sumfixed plays
00{1,4} {2,3}0 vs 5
13{1,2} {3,5}0 vs 4
21{1,5} {2,4}0 vs 3
34{1,3} {4,5}0 vs 2
42{2,5} {3,4}0 vs 1

The residues come out 0, 3, 1, 4, 2: every class in Z5 exactly once, because 2 is a unit mod 5 (2 × 3 ≡ 1). All 15 pairs appear once.

Equation 8 Chromatic index (why odd fields need byes)
χ′(Kn) = n−1  (n even),    n  (n odd)

Read it aloud: "The chromatic index of K n is n minus one when n is even, and n when n is odd."

Say it: "A schedule is an edge coloring, one color per round. Even fields color perfectly in n minus 1 rounds. Odd fields need n rounds and every round leaves exactly one player uncolored: that is the graph theory reason byes exist. The app adds a phantom seat, so each player rests exactly once."

On screen: generate the 13 player example: "13 players with a rotating bye: 13 rounds."

Part 4: the knockout (second run, double elim on the 13 field)

Equation 9 Seeding recurrence seedOrder, seedMeetRound
S(1) = [1],   S(2k) = [s,  2k+1−s  for each s ∈ S(k)]

Read it aloud: "S of one is just seed one. To double the bracket, each seed s gets paired with its mirror, two k plus one minus s."

Say it: "Double the bracket by pairing every seed s with its mirror 2k plus 1 minus s. So S(8) is 1, 8, 4, 5, 2, 7, 3, 6: every first round pair sums to 9, strongest against weakest. The payoff is the subtree isolation lemma: the top 2 to the t seeds land in 2 to the t different subtrees, and two players can only meet at the root of the smallest subtree containing both. That is why seeds 1 and 2 can only ever meet in the final."

On screen: walkthrough Act 3 builds the seed order pass by pass and highlights the meeting round on the tree.

Equation 10 Match counts by loss counting buildBracket, buildDoubleBracket
single: k−1,    double: 2(k−1) + {0 or 1} = 2k−2  or  2k−1

Read it aloud: "Single elimination is k minus one matches. Double elimination is two times k minus one, plus zero or one: so two k minus two, or two k minus one when the grand final resets."

Say it: "Count losses, not matches, every match creates exactly one loss. Single elimination: k minus 1 players must be eliminated once, so k minus 1 matches. Double elimination: every non champion exits with exactly two losses, that is 2k minus 2, plus one more game if the losers champion wins the first grand final and forces the reset."

On screen: the live double elimination run; if the grand final resets you get the 2k minus 1 case live.

Real numbers: k = 8: single is 7 matches, double is 14 or 15 (7 winners bracket + 6 losers bracket + grand final, + reset).

Part 5: the honest limitation (carry-over coda)

Equation 11 Carry-over imbalance carryOverMatrix, carryOverValue
Γ = Σj,k G[j][k]2,    balanced minimum = n(n−1)

Read it aloud: "Gamma equals the sum, over all pairs j and k, of G at j k squared. The balanced minimum is n times n minus one."

Say it: "If I play you right after playing someone strong, you inherit my fatigue, that is a carry-over. G counts every such handoff, and the sum of squares measures how unevenly they pile up. A 2018 theorem proves my own circle method is the WORST possible schedule by this measure. I show that on purpose: the method that gives the one line correctness proof costs you carry-over balance, and stating the tradeoff honestly is part of the design."

On screen: the heatmap in the walkthrough coda, computed live for the current field.

Real numbers: K6: circle method Γ = 60, balanced minimum 6 × 5 = 30, exactly twice as bad. Balanced schedules exist when n is a power of two (Russell 1980).

If you can only own three things cold

1. The congruence proof (Equation 7, the four spoken sentences). This is the one he will ask.

2. K(S − E): upsets pay more because S minus E is nearly 1 when the expectation was low. Points conserved, equal and opposite.

3. Loss counting: every match makes one loss, non champions carry exactly two, so double elimination is 2k − 2, plus 1 on a reset.
